Eight interactive demos for the Optics 1 and Optics 2 OKAP sessions. The tabs are numbered in a suggested order: each demo builds on the far point idea from the one before.

  1. Pick a demo and load a practice problem. Its full statement appears above the controls.
  2. Predict the answer. Turn on predict mode to hide results until you commit.
  3. Reveal and explore. Drag sliders, or use the − and + buttons for exact steps.

Each demo has its own problems, a "How to read this diagram" panel, common mistakes, and check-yourself questions at the bottom.

Seven big ideas
  1. 1. Light bends at surfaces. Toward the normal entering a denser medium; past the critical angle it cannot exit.
  2. 2. Vergences add. U + D = V. Diopters add; distances do not. A tear layer or lens surface is one more term.
  3. 3. Find the far point. Every correction puts the lens's focal point on the eye's far point.
  4. 4. Distance changes the effect. A lens moved away is perceived as more plus, so the required lens goes the other way. Closer Add Plus (required).
  5. 5. Each meridian is its own lens. Write both meridians. The spherical equivalent is their midpoint.
  6. 6. Prisms. Prism = decentration (cm) × power. The image shifts toward the apex.
  7. 7. Magnification is a ratio. Eyepiece ÷ objective, D/4, or m = U / V. The sign gives orientation.
Conventions used throughout
  • Light travels left to right. Distances measured against the direction of light are negative.
  • Vergence = n / distance (m). Real objects give diverging light, so negative vergence. Air (n = 1.00) unless stated.
  • V = U + D. Magnification m = U / V (positive upright, negative inverted).
  • Vertex: d = distance the lens moves, positive toward the eye. Required power Fnew = F / (1 − dF). Perceived power Fperc = F / (1 + dF).
  • Perceived and required power always change in opposite directions.
  • Far and near points (Optics 1) are given as dioptric distances R and P, positive in front of the eye (R = −Rx), as in the lecture.
Formula sheet
  • Snell's law: n sinθ = n′ sinθ′ (angles from the normal). Critical angle: sinθc = n2 / n1.
  • Vergence: V = n / d (m). U + D = V. Magnification m = U / V.
  • Correcting lens: its secondary focal point F′ must coincide with the eye's far point.
  • Accommodation: A = P − R (R = −Rx). Near add = demand − A/2.
  • Retinoscopy: net = gross − working distance (67 cm = 1.50 D by convention; 50 cm = 2.00 D).
  • Vertex: Fnew = F / (1 − dF), d positive toward the eye. Estimate: ΔF ≈ dF².
  • Astigmatism: SE = sphere + cylinder / 2. Interval of Sturm (D) = |cylinder|.
  • Prentice's rule: Δ = h (cm) × D.
  • Telescope: M = Deyepiece / Dobjective; separation = fobj + feye.
  • Magnifier: M = D / 4 (D / 4 + 1 at the eye, accommodating). Kestenbaum add = acuity denominator / numerator.
  • Mirror: D = 2 / r (r in meters).
  • Duochrome: red clearer, add minus; green clearer, add plus.

Far point, near point, and accommodation

Every eye has a far point (relaxed) and a near point (full accommodation). Set the refractive error and amplitude, then move the target and see whether the eye can bring it onto the retina.

Key idea Big idea 3 The far point is where the relaxed eye is focused; the near point is where full accommodation takes it. Amplitude A = P − R.

How to read this diagram
  • Gold lines: light from the target entering the eye.
  • Green dot on the retina: sharp focus. Gold bar on the retina: blur; its height shows how much.
  • Dioptric ruler: diopters in front of the eye. 0 is infinity; values grow as objects come closer (2.5 D = 40 cm).
  • Gray zone at the left: points behind the eye (virtual). An uncorrected hyperope's far point sits here.
  • Green dot: far point. Maroon dot: near point. Green bar between them: range of clear vision. Triangle: the target.
Try this
  1. Load “Frenella: −15 D, 10 D” and predict her far point and near point before revealing.
  2. Slide the target from infinity toward the eye. Find where it turns sharp, then where it blurs again.
  3. Switch to “Full correction at the eye.” The far point moves to infinity; the amplitude stays the same.

R and P are the dioptric distances of the far and near points (positive in front of the eye; R = −Rx). Correction is modeled at the eye (contact lens), so no vertex effect. Focus shifts inside the eye are drawn 4× larger.

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • R = −Rx. Myopes have a far point in front of the eye; hyperopes have one behind it.
  • Accommodation only adds plus, so it can never rescue a target beyond a myope's far point.
  • An uncorrected hyperope spends accommodation just to see at distance, which pushes the near point away.

Check yourself

An uncorrected −4.00 D myope has 6.00 D of amplitude. Where are the far point and near point?

Far point first (idea 3): R = +4 D, so 25 cm. Accommodation adds 6 D: P = 10 D, so 10 cm. Clear from 25 to 10 cm.

An emmetrope with 2.00 D of amplitude wants to read at 40 cm. Keeping half in reserve, what add?

The demand at 40 cm is 2.50 D. She should use only half her 2.00 D (1.00 D), so the add supplies the rest: +1.50 D.

Retinoscopy

The reflex is light leaving the patient's eye and heading for its far point. Against motion means the far point lies between the eye and your peephole; with motion means it lies beyond you or behind the eye. Neutralize, then subtract the working distance.

Key idea Big idea 3 Against motion: the far point is between the eye and you. With motion: it is beyond you or behind the eye. Neutral: it is at your peephole. Net = gross − working distance.

How to read this diagram
  • Left panel, gold lines: light leaving the patient's eye, heading for its far point (green dot).
  • Shaded zone: between the eye and your peephole. A far point here gives against motion.
  • Right panel, faint band: your streak sweeping across the face. Bright band in the pupil: the reflex.
  • Reflex moves the same way as the streak = with. Opposite way = against. Whole pupil glows = neutral.
Try this
  1. Load “−3.00 myope” and predict with or against before revealing.
  2. Add minus in 0.25 D steps. The reflex speeds up, brightens, and widens as you approach neutral.
  3. Press “New mystery patient,” neutralize, write down the net Rx, then press “Show the Rx.”

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • Always subtract the working distance: 67 cm = 1.50 D (by convention), 50 cm = 2.00 D.
  • With = add plus. Against = add minus.
  • The working distance comes off the sphere only, never the cylinder.

Check yourself

At 50 cm you neutralize with +1.00 D. What is the net Rx?

Neutral puts the far point at your peephole, so remove the working distance: +1.00 − 2.00 = −1.00 D.

A −0.50 D myope is examined at 67 cm with no lens. With or against? Why?

With. Its far point (1 / 0.5 D = 2 m) lies beyond your 67 cm peephole.

Duochrome and chromatic aberration

Shorter wavelengths refract more, so green focuses in front of red. The duochrome straddles the retina with the two colors: whichever side focuses closer to the retina looks sharper. No color discrimination is needed, so it works for color-deficient patients.

Key idea Big idea 1, 3 Green focuses in front of red. Red clearer, add minus; green clearer, add plus (RAM-GAP).

How to read this diagram
  • Green, red, and dashed yellow lines: the cone of light for each color converging and diverging.
  • Dots: where each color focuses. Maroon line: the retina. Positions are in diopters, spacing exaggerated.
  • Right panel: the chart as the patient sees it. Each side is blurred according to how far its color focuses from the retina.
Try this
  1. Load “Myope, under-minused” and predict which side looks sharper.
  2. Press “Add −0.25” until the two sides match.
  3. Try “Hyperope, under-plussed.” The rule is identical, because it depends on where the focus sits, not on the type of refractive error.

The eye's longitudinal chromatic aberration spans roughly 2 D across the visible spectrum; the duochrome filters sit about a quarter diopter either side of yellow. Many examiners end on equal clarity or a slight red preference to avoid over-minusing.

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • Green clearer means over-minused. End on equal, or slightly red.
  • The test relies on chromatic aberration, not color vision, so it works for color-deficient patients.

Check yourself

The patient says red is sharper. What next?

Red focuses closest to the retina, so all the foci sit in front of it: under-minused. Add minus (RAM).

You add −0.25 D and green becomes sharper. What happened?

Green now focuses closer than red: the foci moved behind the retina, so you over-minused by about 0.25 D. Go back one step.

Telescopes and magnifiers

An afocal telescope turns parallel light into parallel light at a new angle. A weak objective and a strong eyepiece magnify; swap them and the telescope minifies.

Key idea Big idea 7 Magnification = eyepiece power ÷ objective power. Keplerian: two plus lenses, inverted, longer. Galilean: plus objective and minus eyepiece, upright, shorter.

How to read this diagram
  • Gold lines: a parallel bundle from a distant object on the axis. A narrower bundle leaving the eyepiece means magnification.
  • Maroon line: light from an off-axis object. It leaves at a steeper angle when magnified; leaving on the opposite side means the image is inverted.
  • F′ obj: the objective's focal point. Keplerian rays cross here (real intermediate image); in a Galilean telescope it lies beyond the eyepiece (dashed, virtual).
Try this
  1. Load “Keplerian +5 / +15” and predict the magnification, orientation, and length.
  2. Press “Lenses swapped.” The same pair of lenses now minifies.
  3. Load “Galilean +10 / −30” and compare its length and orientation with the Keplerian.

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • The stronger lens is the eyepiece. Making it the objective builds a reverse telescope.
  • A Galilean eyepiece has a negative focal length, so its tube is shorter: fobj − |feye|.
  • Magnifier ratings vary: this tool uses D/4; some manufacturers label D/4 + 1 as "trade magnification."

Check yourself

+4.00 D objective, +20.00 D eyepiece: type, magnification, orientation, length?

Magnification is eyepiece ÷ objective (idea 7): 20 / 4 = 5×. Two plus lenses: Keplerian, inverted. Length 25 + 5 = 30 cm.

Galilean +8.00 / −24.00: magnification and length? Kestenbaum add for 20/160?

24 / 8 = 3×; the minus eyepiece keeps it upright (Galilean). Length 12.5 − 4.17 = 8.3 cm. Kestenbaum: 160 / 20 = +8.00 D.

Vertex distance and effective power

The patient is fully corrected at the original vertex. Move the lens and watch the focus leave the retina. Then switch to the required lens to bring it back.

Key idea Big idea 3, 4 A lens moved away from the eye is perceived as more plus, so the required power shifts the opposite way. Closer Add Plus (CAP).

How to read this diagram
  • Gold lines: light from a distant object through the lens and into the eye.
  • Dashed lens: the original position. Solid lens: where it is now.
  • Green dot: the eye's far point. Gold dot (Lens F′): the lens focal point. The patient is corrected only when the two coincide.
  • Gold bar on the retina: blur. Green dot on the retina: sharp focus.
Try this
  1. Load “−10 D, 10→20 mm” and predict: does the patient become myopic or hyperopic?
  2. Switch to the required lens and compare its power with the perceived power of the original lens.
  3. Load “−7 D to contact lens” and confirm CAP: the contact needs less minus than the glasses.

A −12.00 D spectacle Rx at 12 mm. What contact lens power do you start with?

Closer Add Plus: the contact needs less minus. The far point is 83.3 + 12 = 95.3 mm from the cornea: −10.49, order −10.50 D.

A +12.00 D lens at 10 mm is refit at 15 mm. What power is now required?

A plus lens moved away is perceived as stronger, so the required lens needs less plus (idea 4). Far point 83.3 − 10 = 73.3 mm behind the cornea; 73.3 + 15 = 88.3 mm: +11.32 D.

Vergence and image formation

Place a real object in front of a thin lens. The three principal rays find the image; dashed lines are virtual extensions. Watch the sign of V and of m.

Key idea Big idea 2, 7 V = U + D. A negative image distance means a virtual image; the sign of m = U / V tells you upright or inverted.

How to read this diagram
  • Maroon arrow: the object. Blue arrow: the image (solid = real, dashed = virtual).
  • Gold lines: the three principal rays. Dashed gray lines: virtual extensions traced backward to a virtual image.
  • F and F′: the primary and secondary focal points. The scale adjusts automatically and is shown at the bottom left.

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • Real objects always give negative U. Dropping the sign is the most common error.
  • Negative image distance = virtual. With this convention m = U / V; negative m = inverted.

Check yourself

+4.00 D lens, object 50 cm away. Image location, type, magnification?

U = −2 and D = +2 give V = +2: a real image 50 cm behind; m = U / V = −1 (inverted, same size). This is the 2f position.

−5.00 D lens, object 20 cm away. Image location, type, magnification?

A minus lens spreads light further: V = −5 − 5 = −10 D, virtual, 10 cm in front; m = U / V = +0.5 (upright, minified).

Conoid of Sturm

An uncorrected spherocylinder focuses light into two perpendicular focal lines. Choose a correction, then scan the viewing plane through the conoid to see the blur change shape. Positions are in diopters relative to the retina.

Key idea Big idea 5 Two focal lines form, each perpendicular to the meridian that creates it. The spherical equivalent puts the circle of least confusion on the retina.

How to read this diagram
  • Two shaded bowties: light in each principal meridian. Colors match the focal line labels.
  • Dots: the two focal lines. Open circle: circle of least confusion. Maroon line: retina. Dashed line: viewing plane.
  • Right panel: the blur shape at the viewing plane, seen from the examiner's side (0° at right, 90° at top).
Try this
  1. Load “+2.00 −3.00 × 180” and predict which focal line lies in front of the retina, and its orientation.
  2. Choose “Spherical equivalent” and confirm the circle of least confusion lands on the retina.
  3. Drag the viewing plane through the conoid: line, circle, line.

Same Rx, uncorrected: where is each focal line and how is it oriented?

The 090 meridian (−1.00) focuses in front of the retina and forms a horizontal line; the 180 meridian (+1.00) focuses behind and forms a vertical line. The circle of least confusion sits on the retina.

Prentice's rule and slab-off

Looking below the optical centers of unequal lenses induces unequal vertical prism. Lower the reading point and watch the images separate. Add slab-off to bring them back together.

Key idea Big idea 6 Prism = decentration (cm) × power. Unequal lenses give unequal prism in downgaze; slab-off goes on the more minus (less plus) lens.

How to read this diagram
  • Red dot: where the eye looks through each lens. Cross: optical center.
  • Triangle: the induced prism. The base points in the base direction (base down = base at the bottom).
  • Right panel: the image each eye sees. Prism shifts each image toward its apex; the vertical gap is the imbalance.
  • Bar: imbalance against the 1.5Δ marker.
Try this
  1. Load “−5.00 / −2.00, 1 cm” and calculate each eye's prism before revealing.
  2. Lower the reading point and watch the two images separate.
  3. Apply slab-off and check which lens receives it.

Use the vertical-meridian power for spherocylinders. Slab-off is generally considered when the imbalance at the reading level exceeds about 1.5Δ.

Common mistakes and self-check

Watch out

  • Below the optical center, minus lenses give base down and plus lenses give base up.
  • Slab-off goes on the lens with more base-down effect in downgaze: the most minus (least plus).

Check yourself

OD +3.00, OS +0.50, reading 8 mm below. Prism, imbalance, slab-off lens?

Plus below center gives base up (idea 6). OD 0.8 × 3 = 2.40Δ, OS 0.40Δ; imbalance 2.00Δ. Slab-off adds base up to the eye with less: OS.

Why does slab-off go on the more minus lens?

It produces the most base-down prism in downgaze; slab-off grinds base-up prism into its lower portion to cancel the excess.
